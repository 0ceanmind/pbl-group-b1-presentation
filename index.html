SLO 1 · Hematopoiesis

A tissue built to make blood.

Red bone marrow is a highly vascular connective tissue responsible for hematopoiesis — the continuous production of blood cells from multipotent hematopoietic stem cells.

Regulated by growth & differentiation factors
Stem cell factor (SCF) Cytokines Erythropoietin

● Red marrow

Active hematopoiesis — in adults, mainly in the axial skeleton.

● Yellow marrow

Much of the remaining marrow — predominantly adipose tissue.

SLO 1 · Microanatomy

Cords between
sinusoids.

The architecture

Red marrow consists of marrow cords separated by vascular sinusoids.

Vascular sinusoids

Wide, thin-walled vessels. Mature blood cells cross the sinusoidal wall to enter the bloodstream.

The marrow cord

Supported by reticular cells and reticulin fibres (type III collagen); contains developing blood cells, macrophages, adipocytes, plasma cells and megakaryocytes.

Erythroblastic island

Erythroid precursors gather around a central macrophage, which supports them and helps regulate their maturation and release.

Megakaryocyte

Lies close to the sinusoid, releasing platelets directly into the circulation.

Entering the blood

Mature red cells cross the sinusoidal wall and join the circulation.

Two organs that inspect the blood — and reclaim what it carries.

SLO 2 · Liver

The hepatic lobule.

Central vein

At the centre of a roughly hexagonal lobule; receives blood from the sinusoids.

Hepatocytes

The main liver cells, arranged in plates or cords.

Hepatic sinusoids

Specialised blood channels between hepatocyte plates.

Portal triad

At the corners: portal vein branch, hepatic artery and bile duct.

Kupffer cells

Macrophages along the sinusoids; remove foreign particles and cellular debris.

Bile canaliculi

Small channels between hepatocytes; collect bile and direct it toward the bile ducts.

SLO 2 · Spleen

Two pulps. One filter.

Surrounded by a connective tissue capsule, the spleen contains two main histological regions.

Capsule & trabeculae

Connective tissue extensions from the capsule provide structural support.

White pulp

Lymphoid tissue rich in lymphocytes, surrounding branches of the splenic artery — immune responses.

Red pulp

Splenic cords and sinusoids — filters blood and removes old or damaged red cells.

SLO 3 · Iron recycling

Inside the red-pulp macrophage.

Erythrophagocytosis

Red pulp macrophages recognise and engulf senescent red cells.

Hemoglobin is split

Globin → amino acids. Heme is kept for its iron.

Heme oxygenase-1

HO-1 degrades heme, releasing free iron. The porphyrin becomes bilirubin.

Ferroportin

Exports iron into plasma — the only known cellular iron exporter.

Transferrin

Carries iron to the bone marrow to build new red cells.

Group B-1 research · StatPearls: Anemia
SLO 3 · The critical regulator

Hepcidin holds the key.

The hormone

  • Hepcidin is produced by the liver.
  • It binds ferroportin on the macrophage, causing its internalization and degradation.
  • Iron is trapped inside the cell; plasma iron falls.

Iron deficiency Our patient

  • Hepcidin is suppressed.
  • Ferroportin remains active on the membrane.
  • More recycled iron is released into plasma.

Inflammation

  • IL-6 stimulates hepcidin.
  • Ferroportin is degraded; iron is trapped in macrophages.
  • Result: the hypoferremia of anemia of inflammation.

SLO 4 · Synthesis of red cells

Building a red cell takes iron.

Hematopoietic stem cells give rise to the erythroid lineage. Iron is essential for heme and hemoglobin synthesis along the way.

Iron-deficient: cells are smaller and contain less hemoglobin — microcytic, hypochromic.

SLO 4 · Breakdown of red cells

Born in the marrow. Retired in the spleen.

≈120 days in circulation

Metabolic systems slow; the membrane loses pliability and becomes fragile.

Rupture & phagocytosis

Old cells rupture in tight spots — especially the red pulp. Hemoglobin is taken up by macrophages: Kupffer cells, spleen, bone marrow.

Iron returns

Transferrin carries iron to the marrow for new red cells, or to the liver and tissues for storage as ferritin.

Pigment leaves

Porphyrin → bilirubin, secreted by the liver into bile.

SLO 4 · Anemia

Anemia: one result, many causes.

A deficiency of hemoglobin in the blood — from too few red cells, or too little hemoglobin in each.

Blood loss

Acute hemorrhage

Plasma is replaced first, diluting the red cell concentration.

Our patient
Blood loss

Chronic hemorrhage

Iron can’t be absorbed fast enough → small, hemoglobin-poor (microcytic, hypochromic) cells.

Marrow failure

Aplastic anemia

Stem cell damage — radiation, chemotherapy, toxins, autoimmune disease; about half idiopathic.

Maturation failure

Megaloblastic anemia

Hemolytic

SLO 4 · Effects on the circulation

Too thin. Too thick.

Normal

Blood viscosity is about 3× that of water. Cardiac output is the reference point.

Anemia — blood too thin

  • Viscosity may fall to ≈1.5× water → less resistance, plus hypoxic vasodilation.
  • Cardiac output can rise to 3–4× normal; on exertion, severe hypoxia and possible heart failure.

Polycythemia vera — blood too thick

  • Viscosity can reach ≈10× water → sluggish flow.
  • Cardiac output stays near normal: high viscosity is offset by a larger blood volume.

SLO 6 · Iron metabolism

Absorb. Export. Carry. Store.

Dietary iron is absorbed mainly in the duodenum. Non-heme iron is reduced to Fe2+ and enters through DMT1.

Inside the intestinal cell, iron is either stored as ferritin or exported into the blood through ferroportin.

In blood, iron binds transferrin, which delivers it to the bone marrow for red cell production, or to tissues for storage as ferritin.

Hepcidin from the liver acts on ferroportin: high hepcidin → less iron enters blood. In iron deficiency, hepcidin decreases → more absorbed and released.

Our patient: prolonged, heavy menstrual bleeding removes iron faster than absorption replaces it — stores are gradually depleted.

So: absorbed in the intestine → exported by ferroportin → carried by transferrin → stored as ferritin, with hepcidin setting the flow.
